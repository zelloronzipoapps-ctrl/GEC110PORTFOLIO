Photo A: Integration in Practice

Artifacts: Aged MSU Gymnasium Seal & Sarimanok Sculpture (College of Fisheries)

If you look closely at the structural landscape of this campus, you’ll find that every brick and cement tells a story about who we are and where we came from. The Mindanao State University System wasn’t built on a whim; it was carved out under Republic Act 1387 through the visionary push of Senator Domocao Alonto as a direct and as a necessary response to the historical "Mindanao Problem." It was conceived as a living social laboratory, a high stakes experiment in peace, national integration, and cultural education across the diverse MINSUPALA region.

Walk past the College of Fisheries, and you’re greeted by the Sarimanok look-alike sculpture. It isn't just an ornamental fixture: it's a legendary Maranao bird clutching a fish, wrapped in traditional Okir motifs. It embeds indigenous Moro heritage directly into our everyday academic scenery. It’s a constant, unapologetic reminder that our education is inextricably bound to the local identity and regional history we inhabit.

Then there’s the old university seal mounted on the gymnasium wall. If you glance at it, you’ll notice the visible wear and tear of the faded paint and weathered edges brought on by time. But to someone who looks past the surface, that physical aging isn't a sign of neglect: it’s a mark of the pure strength and resilience of a university that serves its people. It reflects decades of history, generations of students passing through, and an institution that has stood its ground against changing tides. That aged seal proves that despite the passage of time, the university still holds its core strength, its foundational identity, and an unshakeable commitment to serving the people through education.

Together, the Sarimanok look-alike and that worn university seal act as silent guardians of the original vision. They bridge the gap between historical mandates and current reality, reminding every student walking these grounds that our campus is a sanctuary built for regional peace, cultural preservation, and enduring purpose.

Photo B: Work in Progress

Artifacts: Unpaved Walkway Footprint & Outdoor Basketball Game

Look closely at the physical geography of this campus: a stray footprint stamped into a dusty, unpaved path, alongside a group of kids shooting hoops on an open court. On the surface, it looks like a standard college life. But if you look deeper, through the lens of Mindanao’s history, these everyday scenes reveal the quiet friction between full integration and total absorption.

Basketball courts bring people together, sure. But relying solely on dominant, mainstream pastimes highlights the fragile line between true integration and cold assimilation. Assimilation is the silent pressure put on Moro and Lumad students to abandon their unique heritage, blending into a pre-approved mold just to survive the social landscape. True integration, on the other hand, demands that we safeguard and celebrate indigenous identity while building a shared community. When our collective student life defaults almost entirely to westernized or national pastimes without making real room for traditional games and cultural spaces, it signals an unspoken systemic failure. It tells us that the promise of a peaceful, inclusive academic haven remains a work in progress. Even those unpaved footpaths act as a physical metaphor for the structural gaps that still need fixing to ensure equal access for everyone.

As one of over 70,000 students walking these grounds, I refuse to be a passive bystander taking notes from the sidelines. Being a peace-builder means acting as a real-world trouble shooter using my voice and my work to push back against passive conformity. It means advocating for inclusive campus initiatives, bringing Moro and Lumad stories to the foreground of our collective narrative, and choosing genuine, raw dialogue over easy ground/surface level quiet. We have to ensure this environment doesn't become a place where distinct identities are quietly erased, but rather a space where every individual culture is recognized, respected, and given room to exist.
